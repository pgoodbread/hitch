Most profile advice tells you to "be interesting" and leaves you staring
at an empty bio box. A Tinder profile template fixes that by giving you
the shape first — which photo goes where, how many sentences the bio
needs, what each one is doing — so all you have to do is fill in your
own details.

Below is the template, slot by slot, plus three filled-in versions so
you can see it working on three very different guys. Steal the
structure, not the words.

## Why a Tinder profile template beats writing from scratch

A profile gets judged in a fixed order. Photo one earns the tap. Photos
two through six survive the scroll. The bio gets maybe ten seconds after
that, and only if the photos bought you the time.

That order is the whole reason a template helps. Writing from scratch,
guys tend to pour everything into the bio and treat photos as whatever
was on their camera roll. The template forces the effort where the
decision actually happens — and the first photo
[decides more than the rest combined](/blog/why-your-first-tinder-photo-decides-everything).

It also stops the two failure modes that kill most profiles: saying
nothing, and saying everything. A slot-based structure gives each part
one job and caps how much it can do.

## The photo slots

Six photos, in this order. Each slot has a job. If you don't have a
photo for a slot, leave it out rather than filling it with something
weak — five strong photos beat six with a dud.

**Slot 1 — the clear face shot.** You, alone, face visible, decent
light, looking at the camera. No sunglasses, no hat brim shadow, no
group. This is the only photo most people will ever see.

**Slot 2 — full body.** Not a mirror selfie. Someone else takes it, you
are doing something or at least standing somewhere real. Leaving this
out reads as hiding something.

**Slot 3 — the social proof shot.** You with friends, clearly
identifiable as you, and ideally not the least attractive person in the
frame. Two or three people, not a wall of nine.

**Slot 4 — the hobby shot.** You mid-activity: climbing, cooking, on
stage, at the trailhead. This is the photo that generates openers,
because it hands her something to ask about.

**Slot 5 — the texture shot.** Travel, a dog, a weird hat, something
that adds a second dimension. Optional but useful.

**Slot 6 — one more good face shot,** different setting and outfit from
slot 1. Confirms slot 1 wasn't a fluke.

The full reasoning behind the order — and the photos quietly costing
you matches — is in the [profile pictures guide](/blog/profile-pictures).

## The bio template

Three sentences. That's the whole thing. More than that and you're
writing a résumé; fewer and there's nothing to grab.

> **Line 1 — a specific, concrete detail about your life.**
> **Line 2 — something self-aware, funny or mildly self-deprecating.**
> **Line 3 — an easy invitation or open question.**

Line one establishes you're a real person with an actual life. Line two
proves you don't take yourself too seriously. Line three makes messaging
you the path of least resistance.

The rules that hold it together:

1. **Specific beats generic.** "I love travel" is wallpaper. "Still
   recovering from a three-day hike I was underprepared for" is a
   conversation.
2. **One hook per line.** Don't stack three interests into one sentence.
3. **Never list.** "6'1", engineer, gym, dog lover" is a dating profile
   written as a spreadsheet.

## Three filled-in examples

Same template, three different guys.

**The outdoorsy one**

> I spend most weekends somewhere with no cell service and questionable
> trail markers.
> I own more camping gear than furniture and I'm not fixing that.
> Tell me the best hike within two hours of here and I'll go this
> Saturday.

Line 1 is concrete. Line 2 admits a flaw without apologizing. Line 3 is
an invitation she can answer in four words.

**The career-focused one**

> I design software by day and have been slowly failing to learn guitar
> for two years.
> The guitar is winning.
> Looking for someone to split Sunday breakfast and unhelpful opinions
> about movies with.

Line 1 mentions work without making it the personality. Line 2 does the
self-aware beat in five words. Line 3 proposes an actual, specific date.

**The funny one**

> Professional over-orderer at restaurants — yes, you can have some.
> My dog thinks I'm impressive, which I've decided is enough.
> If you have strong feelings about the best late-night food in this
> city, I'd like to hear them.

Every line is doing double duty: it's funny and it's telling you
something. That's the bar. If a joke doesn't also reveal a detail, cut
it.

## Prompts and interests

Tinder's prompts are the most-skipped part of the template and the
easiest free win, because most guys leave them blank or write one word.

Treat each prompt as a miniature version of the bio: one specific
detail, one bit of personality. Answer three, not all of them.

- Skip the prompts that invite a cliché ("Two truths and a lie" almost
  always produces filler).
- Pick the ones that let you be concrete about a real preference.
- Never answer a prompt with "ask me" — it's the profile equivalent of
  a shrug.

For interests, pick five that are genuinely yours and at least two that
are narrow. "Music" tells her nothing. "Live music" is slightly better.
A specific genre or a festival is something she can react to.

## The fields everyone leaves blank

Job title, school and location sit under your photos and get read more
than guys assume. They're part of the template too.

**Job.** Write what you actually do in plain words, not your title.
"Designs kitchens" beats "Senior Associate, Experience Delivery." If
your job is hard to explain in three words, use the thing about it
people find interesting instead.

**School.** Fine to include, fine to leave off. It does nothing for you
after about 25.

**Location and distance.** If you travel for work or just moved, say so
in the bio rather than leaving her to guess why you're 40 miles away.
Unexplained distance is a quiet left swipe.

None of these three will win you a match on their own. All three can
lose you one by looking careless.

## How to test whether it's working

Change one thing at a time and give it a week. Match rate is noisy over
a day and readable over seven.

Start with the first photo, because it has the largest effect. Swap it,
wait, compare. Then the bio. Then the order of photos two through four.

If nothing moves across three rounds of changes, the problem usually
isn't the wording — it's that the photos are doing less than you think
they are. That's uncomfortable to hear about your own camera roll,
which is exactly why most guys keep rewriting the bio instead.

## Where the template breaks

Three things undo all of it.

**Contradiction between photos and bio.** A bio about spontaneous
weekend trips paired with six photos taken in the same bar reads as
invented. The template only works if the slots agree with each other.

**Copying the examples word for word.** These exact lines are now on a
page a lot of guys are reading. Use the structure, supply your own
details.

**Treating the profile as finished.** Give a new profile a couple of
weeks, then change the first photo and watch what happens to your match
rate. That single swap moves results more than any bio edit.

And if the profile is working but conversations stall, the problem has
moved downstream — that's a
[messaging problem](/blog/ask-girl-out-tinder), not a profile one. If
you're still getting passed over early, it's worth understanding what
[a left swipe actually signals](/blog/swipe-left-meaning) before you
rewrite anything.

## The short version

Six photo slots in a fixed order, with the clear face shot first. Three
bio lines: a concrete detail, a self-aware beat, an easy invitation.
Three prompts answered like mini-bios. That's the whole Tinder profile
template.

The structure isn't the hard part — filling it honestly is. Write the
detail that's actually true about your week, not the one that sounds
impressive. Specific and real beats polished and generic every single
time.
