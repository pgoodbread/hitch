Almost every Hily review you'll find online is written by someone who
gets paid when you install it. That's not a conspiracy, it's just how
dating-app content works, and it's why the same five adjectives —
innovative, AI-powered, refreshing — turn up in all of them.

Here's the less flattering version. Hily is a competently built
dating app with a genuinely interesting matching idea, a pool that's
thin outside big cities, and one of the more aggressive paywalls in
the category. Whether it's worth your time comes down to which of
those three you run into first.

## What Hily actually is

Hily launched in 2017 and the name is short for "Hey, I like you."
It's a swipe-based app, so the core loop will feel familiar within
about four seconds — cards, a like, a pass, a match, a chat.

What it sells as the difference is the matching layer. Instead of
ordering your queue mostly by who's nearby and active, Hily leans on
behavioural signals: who you swipe on, who you message, how long you
linger, what you've answered in the app's quizzes and prompts. The
pitch is that the feed gets smarter the longer you use it.

There's also more profile surface than Tinder gives you. Quizzes,
interests, a video option, and a set of prompts that give people
something to react to beyond a photo. For someone whose appeal
doesn't fit in six pictures, that's a real advantage.

The app skews slightly younger than Zoosk and slightly more
relationship-shaped than Tinder — most people on there are vaguely
looking for something, without being on a clock about it.

## The part every Hily review skips: the paywall

This is the part a Hily review owes you up front, because it's the
part you'll notice on day two.

Hily is free to use in the sense that matching, messaging your
matches, and video chat all work without paying. But the app is
unusually enthusiastic about reminding you that it has a paid tier,
and it has more than one.

There's Hily Premium, there's a higher Premium+ tier, and there's
Elixir, which is sold as the upgraded experience. Prices move around
by region and by whatever promotion you land in, but weekly billing
is the default framing, and weekly billing is how a subscription that
looks like fifteen dollars quietly becomes sixty a month.

On top of the subscriptions, boosts and profile unblurs are sold
separately — including to people who are already paying. That's the
same twice-asking
trick [Zoosk runs](/blog/zoosk-reviews) with its coins, and it's
worth recognising it for what it is: the subscription doesn't buy you
out of the upsells, it just moves you to a better seat in the same
room.

Cancelling is also not as direct as it should be. If you sign up for
anything, do it through your App Store or Play Store subscription
settings so you can kill it in two taps rather than hunting for a
button inside the app.

## Is the matching actually smarter?

Partly, and it depends on how much you use it.

The behavioural model needs data. If you install the app, swipe
through forty cards in one evening and uninstall it on Thursday, you
have given it nothing to work with and your feed will look like every
other app's feed. People who try Hily for a weekend and report that
the AI is marketing fluff are, in fairness, reviewing a version of
the product that never got to run.

Give it a couple of weeks of real use and the queue does tighten up.
It gets noticeably better at reading the gap between who you say you
want and who you actually swipe on, which is a gap most people have
and nobody admits to.

What it can't do is manufacture people. In a thin market, a smarter
sort of a small pool is still a small pool, and that's the single
biggest predictor of whether you'll like this app. In a major metro
it feels like a real dating app. Two hours outside one, you'll hit
the end of the deck in a few days and the clever matching becomes
irrelevant.

The fake-profile question comes up in every thread about Hily. There
is some of it, as there is everywhere, but it's not notably worse
than the mainstream apps — the verification system catches a decent
share and the rest follows the usual pattern. If a profile is four
professional photos and an immediate push to another platform, it
isn't a person.

## Free versus paid: what you actually get

The free tier is genuinely usable, which is more than you can say for
some competitors. You can match, you can message, you can video chat.

Paying mostly buys the familiar list: unlimited likes, seeing who
already liked you, advanced filters, and an incognito mode. Useful
things, none of them revolutionary, and all of them things you can
get on half a dozen other apps for similar money.

The one that tempts people is seeing who liked you. It feels like it
should save time. In practice it does two things: it tells you the
volume of interest you're currently generating, and it lets you skip
the queue to act on it. If that number is low, the subscription has
just bought you an expensive piece of bad news — and the fix for a
low number isn't a paid tier, it's the profile.

That's the same ceiling [The League](/blog/the-league-dating) and
[the Upward dating app](/blog/upward-dating-app) both hit, and it's
why [the Tinder Insights review](/blog/tinder-insights-review)
reaches the same conclusion about paying for analytics: the data is
only worth money if you're going to change something with it.

## Who Hily is good for

Sign up if you're in or near a decent-sized city, you're in your
twenties or early thirties, and you want something a bit more
considered than Tinder without the curated gatekeeping of a
members-only app.

It's also a reasonable fit if you're more interesting in conversation
than in photographs. The quizzes and prompts give you room to be
funny or specific in a way a pure photo app doesn't, which is the
same reason [Coffee Meets Bagel](/blog/coffee-meets-bagel-review)
suits a certain kind of person better than the swipe mills do.

Use it free for two or three weeks before you consider paying. You
need that long for the matching to mean anything, and you need the
match volume data to know whether a subscription would be buying you
reach or just a mirror.

## Who should skip it

Skip it if you're outside a metro area. The pool thins fast and
nothing in the product compensates for that.

Skip it if you're allergic to upsell prompts, because you will see a
lot of them.

And skip it if you're app-shopping because your results are bad. This
is the honest centre of every review on this site: the app is almost
never the variable. The same photos and the same first line produce
roughly the same outcome on Hily, Tinder, Zoosk or anything launching
next spring. If matches aren't arriving,
[your photos are the problem](/blog/profile-pictures), and if you've
already run this loop a few times,
[the diagnostic here](/blog/why-am-i-not-getting-matches-on-tinder)
is a faster read than another signup.

For a wider look at how the mainstream options compare before you
commit to any of them,
[the comparison here](/blog/popular-dating-apps) covers the field.

## The short version

Hily is a better app than its marketing makes it sound and a worse
deal than its pricing makes it look.

The matching is real but slow to earn, the free tier is usable, the
paid tiers are priced weekly in a way that flatters the sticker and
not your bank statement, and the whole thing lives or dies on whether
there are enough people near you.

Worth installing free if you're in a city and curious. Worth paying
for only once you've seen your own match volume and decided you're
buying reach rather than reassurance. Not worth either if the profile
hasn't been finished yet — no amount of behavioural matching rescues
a first photo that isn't doing its job.
