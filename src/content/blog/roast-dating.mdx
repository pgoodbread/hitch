Roast dating is the best known of the profile-review services: you
upload your photos, an AI scores them, and you get told which ones to
keep, which to cut, and roughly why. The promise is that someone
finally tells you the thing your friends won't.

That's a real gap, and the product fills part of it. The part it
doesn't fill is the part most people are actually paying to solve, so
it's worth being precise about which is which before you spend
anything.

## What roast dating actually is

Strip the branding off and it's three things stacked together.

**A photo scorer.** You upload a set, each one comes back with a
number and a short read on what's working and what isn't. This is the
core of the product and the reason people sign up.

**A profile critique.** Bio, prompts, the order your photos sit in.
Lighter than the photo side, more generic in what it tells you.

**A set of rewrite suggestions.** Alternative bio lines, opener ideas,
occasional nudges about what kind of photo you're missing.

The format is fast. You're not waiting days for a human to get back to
you, which is the main thing it has over the older done-for-you
services. You get numbers in minutes and you can re-run it after a
reshoot.

## What the photo score is really measuring

This is the part worth understanding, because everything else follows
from it.

The score is a model's prediction of how a photo is generally received
— trained on ratings, on swipe-style data, on patterns that hold
across a lot of people. It's a decent proxy for the average reaction
of an average viewer.

What it isn't is your match data. A model has no idea who you're
trying to attract, what your city's pool looks like, or whether the
photo that scores a 6 is the one that gets you the conversations you
actually want. A gym mirror shot and a photo of you mid-laugh at a
friend's wedding can land close together on a generic score and be
worlds apart in who they pull.

So treat the number as a smoke alarm, not a verdict. It's very good at
catching the photo that's objectively hurting you — bad lighting, a
crop that hides your face, the group shot where nobody can tell which
one you are. It's much weaker at telling you which of your three
decent photos should lead.

That distinction matters because the ordering question is usually
where the matches are won or lost. [Your first photo decides
almost everything](/blog/profile-pictures), and a generic scorer
picking it for you is a coin flip dressed up as data.

## Where it's genuinely good

**It's blunt, and bluntness is the scarce thing.** The feedback loop
most people have is friends who like them. Friends are structurally
incapable of telling you that your favourite photo is bad. A model
doesn't care, and that alone makes it useful.

**It catches the obvious killers fast.** Sunglasses in every shot.
Five photos that are all the same angle in the same room. The one with
your ex cropped out badly. These are cheap to fix and expensive to
leave, and you'll find them in the first pass.

**It's a reasonable reality check on a shortlist.** If you've got
eight photos and no idea which six to use, running them through and
cutting the bottom two is a sensible use of twenty minutes.

**You can re-run it.** After a reshoot, you get a before and after.
Most review services don't give you that without paying again at full
price.

## Where it falls short

**The bio and prompt advice is thin.** It's the weaker half of the
product by a distance. The suggestions tend toward safe and
interchangeable — the kind of line that reads fine and says nothing. A
bio works when it gives someone a specific thing to grab onto, and
generic polish is the opposite of that. The [worked examples in the
dating profile bio piece](/blog/dating-profile-bio) will get you
further than a generated line will.

**It can't diagnose the thing you're probably asking about.** People
buy a roast because they're not getting matches. A photo score tells
you whether your photos are good. It doesn't tell you whether your
problem is photos at all — it could be your distance settings, a thin
local pool, a [shadowbanned account](/blog/why-am-i-not-getting-matches-on-tinder),
or the fact that you're swiping right on everyone and the algorithm
has noticed. The service will happily grade your photos either way.

**The upsell pressure is real.** The entry point is cheap, and the
flow is built to move you up from it. That's normal pricing, not a
scam, but go in knowing the advertised price is the floor and not the
number most people end up paying.

**Scores drift.** Re-running the same photo can give you a different
number. The direction is usually stable — a bad photo stays bad — but
treat a two-point gap as noise rather than a finding.

## What it costs

Pricing moves and varies by package, so check the site rather than
trusting a number in an article, this one included. The shape is
consistent though: a low entry price for a basic photo pass, and
meaningfully more for the full profile treatment and the rewrite
features.

The honest framing is that the cheap tier is the one that delivers
most of the value. The photo scoring is what the product is good at,
and it's what the entry price buys. The expensive tiers add more of
the part that's weaker.

## Is roast dating worth it

For a specific person, yes.

It's worth it if you have photos you're unsure about, no honest second
opinion available, and you want a fast read before you commit a set to
your profile. Twenty minutes and a small amount of money to find out
that your lead photo is the problem is a good trade. That's the job it
does well.

It's not worth it if you're hoping it tells you why you're not getting
matches. That's a different question with a lot of possible answers,
and a photo scorer is only equipped to rule out one of them.

It's also not worth it before you've done the free work. There's a
decent amount of it, and it moves the number more than a review will.

## Fix these before you pay anyone

Make sure your face is clearly visible in the first photo, alone, in
daylight. That single change outperforms most paid advice.

Cut every duplicate. If two photos tell the same story, keep the
better one and use the slot for something different — a different
setting, a different version of you.

Have one photo that shows you doing something a person could ask about.
Not a hobby humblebrag, just a visible hook.

Write a bio that names something specific. Two concrete details beat a
paragraph of adjectives, every time.

Then check that the app itself isn't the problem. If the pool where
you are is genuinely thin, no amount of photo scoring fixes it, and
the answer is [a different app](/blog/popular-dating-apps) rather than
a better profile.

Once those are done, a roast tells you something you couldn't have
worked out yourself. Before they're done, it mostly tells you things
you already knew and charges you for the privilege.

## How it compares to the alternatives

Against the done-for-you services — the ones where a human rewrites
your profile and sometimes runs your messages — roast dating is faster
and much cheaper, and it gives you less. You're buying a score, not a
strategy.

Against asking a friend who'll actually be honest, it wins on
honesty and loses on context. The friend knows who you're trying to
attract. The model knows what the average person clicks on.

Against doing nothing, it wins comfortably, which is the real
comparison for most people buying it.

And it belongs to the same category as the dating products we've
looked at before — [the League](/blog/the-league-dating),
[Upward](/blog/upward-dating-app) — where the thing being sold is a
shortcut past the work. The products are fine. The shortcut isn't
quite there in any of them.

## The short version

Roast dating is a fast, blunt, reasonably accurate photo scorer with a
weaker profile-writing product attached. The photo feedback is worth
the entry price if you genuinely can't tell which of your photos are
good. The bio suggestions are not worth upgrading for.

Buy it to settle a photo question you can't settle yourself. Don't buy
it expecting a diagnosis — do the free fixes first, and if the matches
still don't come, the problem is somewhere a photo score was never
going to look.
