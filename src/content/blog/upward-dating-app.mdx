The Upward dating app is Match Group's Christian app — same company
that owns Tinder, built for people who want faith to be part of the
filter rather than something that comes up awkwardly on date three.
It swipes like every other app you've used. What's different is who
is on it and what the profile asks you about.

Whether it's worth your time comes down to one question, and it isn't
"is the app good." It's whether a smaller pool of people who share a
specific thing with you beats a huge pool where you have to find that
out one conversation at a time.

## What the Upward dating app actually is

Upward launched under Match Group, which is the parent company behind
Tinder, Hinge, OkCupid, and most of the other names you've heard. That
matters for two reasons.

The good one: it's a real product with real engineering behind it, not
a two-person app that will quietly stop getting updates. The swipe
mechanics, the reporting tools, the photo handling — all of it works
the way you expect, because it's built on top of a stack that has been
handling dating traffic for a decade.

The less good one: Match Group apps share a business model. The free
tier is deliberately a little frustrating, and the paid tier sells you
reach and information. If you've hit a swipe cap on Tinder and felt the
nudge toward Gold, you already know the shape of it.

Upward's actual differentiator is the profile. Alongside the normal
photos and prompts, it asks about your denomination, how often you go
to church, and how central faith is to your life. Those answers are
visible on the profile and usable as filters. That's the whole product
thesis: move the compatibility question to the top of the funnel
instead of the third date.

## Who it's for

Upward works for you if faith is a genuine filter rather than a
background detail. If "does she go to church" is a question you'd
otherwise have to work into a conversation, an app that answers it
before you swipe saves you real time.

It also works if you're in or near a decent-sized city. This is the
part nobody says out loud about niche apps: the smaller the pool, the
more your location decides everything. In a metro area you'll have
enough profiles to be selective. In a small town you can run out in a
weekend, and running out is a worse experience than a mediocre app
with depth.

## Who it isn't for

If you're culturally Christian but not practicing, you're going to
have an odd time. The profile fields push you to state a level of
involvement, and picking one you don't really mean is how you end up
on dates with a mismatch you created yourself. People on this app
generally take those answers at face value.

And if you're looking for something casual, look elsewhere. Not
because the app forbids it, but because the stated intent skews the
other way and you'll spend your energy swimming against it. The
volume plays are the big apps — that's what they're built for.

## How the app works day to day

The loop is familiar. You build a profile, you swipe, you match, you
message. Nothing here will surprise you if you've used a dating app in
the last five years.

The differences are in the details:

**Faith fields are front and centre.** Denomination, church attendance,
how important faith is to you. Fill these in honestly. They're the
reason you're on this app, and leaving them blank makes your profile
read as someone who downloaded it by accident.

**Prompts do a lot of the work.** Like Hinge-style apps, your written
answers carry more weight than they do on Tinder, where photos decide
almost everything. That's an opportunity — a good prompt answer is a
much cheaper edge than a better jawline. The craft is the same as
anywhere: be specific, be reactive, give the other person something to
grab onto. The worked examples in
[the "dating me is like" piece](/blog/dating-me-is-like) transfer over
directly.

**Photos still decide the swipe.** Faith fields and prompts are why
someone keeps reading. The first photo is why they look at all. Nothing
about a niche app changes that, and the
[first-photo rules](/blog/profile-pictures) are identical here.

## Free versus paid

Upward's free tier lets you build a profile, swipe, match, and message.
The paid tier does what paid tiers at Match Group do: lifts the daily
swipe cap, shows you who already liked you, and gives you periodic
visibility boosts.

Prices shift constantly by region and by age bracket, so check the app
rather than trusting a number in an article — including this one.

The more useful thing to say is when it's worth paying, and the logic
is the same one that applies to
[Tinder Gold](/blog/is-tinder-gold-worth-it). Paid features multiply a
rate you already have. If a hundred people see your profile and two
swipe right, paying to be seen by three hundred gets you six. If your
profile isn't converting, you've bought a larger audience for the same
result.

On a niche app there's one wrinkle that cuts the other way. When the
pool is small, seeing who already liked you is worth more than it is
on Tinder, because you're not filtering a firehose — you're making sure
you don't miss the handful of people who are actually a fit. That's a
real argument for paying here that doesn't hold up on the big apps.

Still: fix the profile first, then decide. Doing it in that order means
you're multiplying something worth multiplying.

## How it compares to the big apps

Upward will feel thin if you're coming from Tinder. Fewer profiles,
slower match pace, longer gaps. That's the trade, not a bug.

What you get back is a higher hit rate per match. On a general-purpose
app, a match is the start of a screening process — you still have to
find out what they want, whether they're serious, and whether the
thing that matters most to you matters to them at all. On Upward, a
chunk of that screening is done before the conversation starts.

Which one wins depends on what your bottleneck is. If you're getting
plenty of matches and none of them go anywhere, a filtered pool is
exactly the fix. If you're not getting matches at all, switching apps
won't help — the problem travels with you, because it's the profile.
That's worth being blunt about: people download a new app hoping for a
reset and get the same result three weeks later with less volume.

A quick way to tell which camp you're in: if your matches dry up after
the first message, the issue is your
[opening messages](/blog/tinder-openers), not the platform. If they
never start, it's the photos and the bio, and running through a
[profile template](/blog/tinder-profile-template) will move that
further in an afternoon than any app switch.

## The short version

The Upward dating app is a competently built Christian dating app from
the company that builds most of them. The faith fields are the product,
and they work — you get a smaller pool that's pre-filtered for the
thing that would otherwise take you three dates to establish.

Download it if faith is a real filter for you and you live somewhere
with enough people to make a niche pool viable. Skip it if you're
hoping a new app fixes a match rate the old one couldn't, because it
won't — and on a smaller pool, a weak profile has fewer chances to get
lucky.

If you want a hand on that part, the
[tools that claim to write your profile for you](/blog/rizz-app) do one
narrow job well and several others badly. Worth knowing which is which
before you pay for one of those, too.
