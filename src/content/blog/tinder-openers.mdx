Most advice about Tinder openers hands you a list and wishes you luck.
The list is the problem. She has seen the list — the same four openers
are sitting in her inbox right now from four different guys, and she
answered none of them.

What follows is the method instead: how to build a first message out of
her actual profile, where to look when the profile is thin, and how long
to wait before you accept the silence. It takes about thirty seconds per
match once you have the shape down.

## Why most Tinder openers get ignored

A woman with a reasonably good profile gets more first messages in a
week than most guys get in a year. She is not reading them. She is
scanning them, and the scan is looking for one thing: is this addressed
to me, or was it addressed to anybody?

"Hey" fails that test. So does "how's your weekend going," and so does
the clever line you found on a list, because clever-and-generic still
lands in the anybody pile. The smoother it sounds, the more obviously
reusable it is.

This is the split between this page and a
[list of pick up lines](/blog/pick-up-line) or a set of
[rizz lines to copy](/blog/best-rizz-lines). Those are useful once you
know which of her details you are reacting to. They are the wording.
The wording is the easy half.

## React to something only she has

One rule underneath everything else: your opener should be impossible to
send to anyone else.

That is a real test, not a slogan. Write your message, then ask whether
you could paste it to the next three matches without editing. If yes,
delete it. If it would read as nonsense to anyone but her, you have
something.

It works because it proves effort without announcing effort. You did not
tell her you read her profile. You demonstrated it in eight words, and
she can tell the difference instantly.

## The three-part opener

Almost every first message that works has the same shape.

**1. The observation.** Name the specific thing you noticed. Her third
photo, the line in her bio about being bad at parallel parking, the
prompt answer that was funnier than it needed to be.

**2. The angle.** Have a take on it. Agree, disagree, be mildly
suspicious of it, claim expertise you do not have. This is where
personality enters. An observation with no angle is just a caption.

**3. The easy exit.** End somewhere she can answer in a handful of
words. Not an interview question, not something requiring a paragraph.
The bar is: could she reply usefully while waiting for coffee?

Put together:

> Ambitious of you to put the ski photo second when the first one is
> clearly taken at a wedding. Are you actually good or is it the one
> trip?

Observation, angle, easy exit. Ten seconds of work, and it could not be
sent to anybody else on the app.

A weaker version of the same instinct:

> Nice ski photo, do you ski a lot?

Same detail, no angle, and a question she can answer with one word. It
is not offensive. It just gives her nothing to push against, which is
how a conversation dies on message two.

## Where to find the hook

Work down the profile in this order. Stop at the first thing you have an
actual reaction to.

- **Prompt answers.** The best source by a distance. She wrote them
  voluntarily, which means she wants a response to them. Most guys skip
  straight past.
- **Photo four or five.** Not the first photo. Complimenting her lead
  shot reads as "I looked at your face and stopped," which is what every
  other message says. The later photos are where the dog, the summit,
  the terrible karaoke night live.
- **A specific line in the bio.** One line, not the whole bio. Pick the
  oddest detail, not the most impressive one.
- **Job or location.** Weakest of the four, but workable if something
  about it is unusual or if she has just moved.

Notice the pattern: you are looking for the thing she chose to include
that was not strictly necessary. That is the part she is interested in
talking about.

## Tinder openers for a profile with nothing in it

Four photos, empty bio, no prompts. It happens constantly, and it is the
situation where guys fall back on "hey" hardest.

Two options that beat it.

**Guess something, wrongly and specifically.** "You have the exact
energy of someone who has strong opinions about airport arrival times."
It is playful, it is clearly written for her, and being wrong is fine —
being wrong is the thing she corrects, and a correction is a reply.

**React to the absence.** "Four photos, no bio, unbelievable
confidence. I respect it." This only works if it is warm rather than
snide, and it works more often than it should.

What does not work is asking her to do the labour: "tell me about
yourself" puts the whole conversation on her side of the table before
she has any reason to carry it.

## Timing, and how long to wait

Send it when you match, or within the first few hours. Matches go cold
fast — she may have swiped through forty profiles that evening and will
not remember yours by Thursday.

Length: one or two sentences. Long first messages read as invested
before you have any reason to be, and invested-too-early is a specific
turn-off rather than a neutral one.

Then leave it alone. One opener, no follow-up bump, no "?" two days
later. If she has not replied, she has read it and passed — the honest
version of what
[Tinder's read receipts](/blog/does-tinder-have-read-receipts) would
tell you if you paid for them. Double-texting a stranger converts almost
never and costs you the chance to look relaxed.

## When it lands

Your opener worked and she replied. The job now changes: match her
length and keep asking about the thread she picked up, not the one you
planned.

Two or three exchanges in, while the conversation still has energy, move
toward [asking her out](/blog/ask-girl-out-tinder). The most common way
a good opener gets wasted is fifty messages of pleasant nothing until
one of you stops replying.

If chats reliably die at message three, the opener is not your problem.
Something further along is.

## When good openers keep failing

Here is the uncomfortable part. If you are sending specific, well-built
openers and getting nothing back across dozens of matches, the openers
are not what is broken.

A first message cannot do much on its own. She reads it, taps your
profile, and decides there — which means the reply rate is mostly a
profile number, not a messaging number. Guys spend weeks rewriting
openers to fix something that lives two steps upstream.

Check that first:

- The [first photo](/blog/why-your-first-tinder-photo-decides-everything)
  does most of the deciding, and swapping it moves results more than any
  wording change.
- A bio that hands her something to react to makes her replies easier
  too. The [bios that work](/blog/best-tinder-bios-for-guys) are the
  ones with hooks in them.
- If the whole profile needs structure rather than edits, start from the
  [Tinder profile template](/blog/tinder-profile-template) and fill the
  slots.

It is also worth knowing what a pass actually means before you read too
much into it — most of them are
[not about you](/blog/swipe-left-meaning) in any way you could have
changed with a better sentence.

## The short version

Good Tinder openers are not lines, they are reactions. Find the one
detail she chose to include, have a take on it, and end somewhere she
can answer in five words. Send it early, keep it to two sentences, and
send it once.

If that stops working, stop editing the message. The opener is the last
five percent of the job — the profile did the other ninety-five before
she ever read a word.
