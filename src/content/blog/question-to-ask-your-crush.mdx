Every question to ask your crush list on the internet is the same list:
a hundred questions, no order, no explanation, half of them things you
would only ask someone you'd already dated for a year. You scroll,
pick one at random, send it, and get back "haha idk".

The question isn't the problem. The fit is. A question works when it's
easy to answer, specific enough that it couldn't have been sent to
anyone else, and pitched at the depth the conversation has actually
earned. Everything below is grouped by that last part — where you are,
not what mood you're in.

## What makes a good question to ask your crush

Four things, roughly in order of how much they matter.

- **It's open-ended.** "Do you like hiking?" ends in one word. "What's
  the best place you've hiked?" hands them a story. Yes/no questions
  make the other person do the work of restarting the conversation.
- **It's specific to them.** A question built from something in their
  profile or something they said ten minutes ago proves you were
  paying attention. Generic questions prove the opposite.
- **It's easy to answer.** The best questions have an obvious first
  sentence. If they have to think for thirty seconds before typing,
  they'll reply later, which usually means never.
- **It matches the depth so far.** "What's your biggest fear?" on day
  one isn't deep, it's uncomfortable. Depth is something you build,
  not something you open with.

Everything else — funny, flirty, weird — is a flavour on top of those
four. Get the four right and almost any flavour works.

## Questions to ask your crush over text

Early texting has one job: prove the conversation is worth having.
Keep it light, keep it about them, and give them something they can
answer in one breath.

> What's the most unnecessary thing you own and genuinely love?

> Be honest — are you a "reply within two minutes" person or a
> "three days later, sorry, just saw this" person?

> What's something you're weirdly good at that never comes up?

> If your week had a title, what would this one be called?

A good reply here is anything longer than four words, ideally with a
detail attached. That detail is your next question — you don't need a
new one from a list, you need to ask about the thing they just told
you.

If you're still at the stage where the chat hasn't started at all, the
question is really an opener, and openers follow their own rules. Our
guide to [pick up lines](/blog/pick-up-line) covers what to send first;
this article is about what comes after that.

## Questions that use their profile

These are the highest-hit-rate questions there are, and they're not on
any list, because they're built from one person's profile.

> You've got a photo in a kayak and a bio that says you hate being
> outside. I need this explained.

> Two concert photos and a dog. Which one is the bigger part of your
> life, honestly?

> Your bio says you'll judge my coffee order. Oat flat white. How bad
> is it?

The structure is always the same: name a detail, react to it, ask one
thing. Thirty seconds of reading gets you a question nobody else in
their inbox is asking. If you want to see what a profile actually
gives you to work with, reading through
[good bios](/blog/best-tinder-bios-for-guys) is a decent way to train
the habit.

## Questions to go a bit deeper

Once you've had a few exchanges and the replies are getting longer,
you can raise the stakes. Not "what's your trauma" — just questions
that ask for an opinion instead of a fact.

> What's something everyone around you seems to enjoy that you just
> don't get?

> What would you do with a completely free year — no money worries, no
> one watching?

> Who in your life do you text first when something good happens?

> What's a thing you changed your mind about in the last couple of
> years?

A good sign here is that they ask you the same question back. That's
the conversation becoming mutual, which is the whole point. If they
give you a two-word answer, step back a level — the depth wasn't there
yet, and that's fine.

## Flirty questions that aren't creepy

Flirty works when it's about who they are rather than what they look
like. The dividing line is simpler than people make it: a compliment
about their character invites a reply, a compliment about their body
ends the thread.

> What's the fastest way someone could impress you, and how obvious is
> it when they're trying?

> You seem like trouble in a very organised way. Where does that come
> from?

> What's your most controversial takeaway-food opinion, and are you
> prepared to defend it?

> If we ended up on a terrible first date, what's the most likely
> reason?

That last one does double duty — it's playful, and it puts the idea of
a date in the conversation without actually asking yet.

What doesn't work is the backhanded version — the "you're cute for
someone who…" school of flirting. It reads as a warning sign now, not
a challenge, and it's worth understanding
[why negging backfires](/blog/negging) if you've been told otherwise.

## Questions before a first date

Once a date is on the table, questions should get practical. You're
looking for logistics and preferences, not more personality.

> Are you a "loud bar" person or a "we should be able to hear each
> other" person?

> Is there anything you've been meaning to try that we could just go
> do?

> How far are you willing to travel for good food? Establishing
> parameters.

These do something useful: they make the invitation feel like a
continuation of the chat rather than a separate ask. When you're ready
to make it explicit, our guide to
[asking her out on Tinder](/blog/ask-girl-out-tinder) covers the
timing and the phrasing.

## Questions that flop

- **Interview questions.** "What do you do? Where are you from? How
  many siblings?" Three in a row and it stops being a conversation.
- **Yes/no questions.** They're not wrong, they're just a dead end.
  Add "what" or "why" and the same question suddenly works.
- **Questions you don't care about the answer to.** People can tell.
  The tell is that you don't follow up on anything they said.
- **Anything sexual too early.** The most common message in their
  inbox and the easiest one to leave unanswered.
- **Deep questions with no build-up.** Heavy questions from a stranger
  feel like a test rather than interest.

## What to do when a question lands flat

It happens. You send something you liked and get back "idk lol". Two
options, and neither is sending another question immediately.

Answer it yourself first. "Fair. Mine's a rice cooker I use twice a
year and would fight someone over." You've now given them something to
react to instead of a second thing to produce. Most flat replies are
low effort, not low interest — people mirror the energy in front of
them.

If two or three in a row land flat, that's information. The thread is
over, and pushing harder makes it worse. Move on; there's no question
clever enough to fix a conversation that isn't happening. The same
skill under a newer name is covered in our
[rizz guide](/blog/rizz) if you want the shorter version.

## The takeaway

The best question to ask your crush is open-ended, built from
something specific about them, easy to answer, and pitched at the
depth you've actually reached. Texting questions stay light, profile
questions do the heavy lifting, and depth is something you earn a few
exchanges in.

And if the conversations aren't the problem — if there's barely anyone
to ask in the first place — that's a match problem, not a question
problem. An [AI profile review](/) grades your photos, their order and
your bio together and tells you which part is costing you matches.
