Almost every list of the best dating app for hookups is the same list
in a different order, written by someone who gets paid per signup.
The apps are ranked, the ranking is arbitrary, and the one at the top
is whichever one pays best that quarter.

Here's the version without the affiliate maths. There are maybe four
apps worth your time for casual dating, they're good at genuinely
different things, and the one that works for you depends on where you
live and what your profile looks like — not on which one a blog put
at number one.

## There is no single best dating app for hookups

The uncomfortable part first: for most people, app choice is not the
bottleneck.

Casual dating apps are photo-led and density-driven. Whether you do
well on one comes down to two things: how many people are on it
within a reasonable distance of you, and whether your first photo
clears the bar. Switching apps changes the first thing. It does
nothing at all about the second, which is why the install-a-new-app
reset so rarely works.

So the honest framing is not "which app is best" but "which app has
people near me, and does it suit how I come across". Density first,
fit second. That ordering matters more than any ranking.

## Tinder: the default, and usually the right one

Tinder is the biggest pool in most places, and size is the whole
product. In a city of any size you will see more profiles in an
evening than a curated app shows you in a month.

For casual intent it has one genuinely useful feature that most
people ignore: the "looking for" field. A meaningful share of users
state what they want — something casual, a relationship, still
figuring it out — before you ever match. That turns the problem from
persuading someone into finding the people whose stated intent
already lines up with yours. Those are very different jobs and only
one of them works.

Where Tinder disappoints is signal. A match tells you nothing except
that your lead photo passed. Everything else you find out one
conversation at a time, and a lot of those conversations die.

It's also the most photo-brutal app of the lot, because the decision
window is about a second long. If you take one thing from this piece,
make it that: on Tinder the [first photo decides
everything](/blog/profile-pictures), and nothing else on your profile
gets looked at until it does its job.

The Tinder-specific version of all this — intent signalling, timing,
what actually moves the needle — is in [Tinder
hookups](/blog/tinder-hookups).

## Feeld: the one that's actually built for it

Feeld is the only mainstream app on this list where casual and
non-monogamous intent is the default assumption rather than something
you're hinting at.

The advantage is that nobody has to be coy. People state what they're
looking for, including arrangements the other apps have no vocabulary
for, and you skip the entire dance of working out whether you're on
the same page. For people who find that dance exhausting, it's a
relief.

The cost is density. Outside large cities the pool thins out fast,
and a well-matched app with nobody on it loses to a blunt app with
everybody on it. Check it for a week before you commit to it — if the
queue runs dry in two evenings, that's your answer.

## Hinge: better than its reputation here

Hinge markets itself as the serious one, which puts people off when
they're not after serious. In practice a lot of casual dating happens
on it, and it has a real structural advantage: prompts.

If you write better than you photograph, Hinge gives you somewhere to
put that. Likes land on a specific prompt rather than on your whole
profile, so conversations start with something to react to instead of
"hey". That's a much better starting position than a blank Tinder
match.

The trade-offs are pace and mismatch. You get a handful of profiles a
day, so a quiet week feels much worse, and the median user there is
further toward relationship intent than on Tinder. If you're clear
about what you want, that's fine — it just means more of your likes
go nowhere.

## Grindr: in a category of its own

For men seeking men, Grindr is the honest answer and has been for
over a decade. It's grid-based rather than swipe-based, proximity is
the organising principle, and intent is stated bluntly and early.

It is a different product from everything else here, and comparing it
on the same axes doesn't really work. If it applies to you, you
already know. If it doesn't, the rest of this list is your field.

## What about the apps marketed as hookup apps?

Be careful here. The sites that advertise themselves specifically as
hookup or affair apps are, as a category, the worst value on the
market: heavy paywalls, thin real user bases, and a long history of
inflating activity with accounts that aren't going anywhere.

The reliable pattern is the opposite of what the ads suggest. Casual
dating mostly happens on the big mainstream apps, used with clear
intent. An app whose entire marketing pitch is the outcome rather
than the experience is usually selling the pitch.

## How to actually pick one

Three questions, in this order.

**Where do you live?** In a dense city, you can afford to optimise
for fit, and Feeld or Hinge become real options. Outside one, density
wins and that usually means Tinder. There's no cleverness available
when the queue is empty.

**Do you photograph well or write well?** Photo-led people do better
on Tinder and Grindr. Writers do better on Hinge, where there's
somewhere for the writing to land. Playing to the wrong one is the
most common self-inflicted problem.

**How direct do you want to be?** If you want intent stated upfront
with no interpretation required, Feeld and Grindr are built for that.
On Tinder and Hinge you'll be reading signals and stating your own
politely.

Then pick one app, not four. Activity is a ranking input nearly
everywhere, and running four profiles at half-attention means four
accounts the algorithms deprioritise. One app, properly set up, for a
month.

## The part that isn't the app

Whatever you pick, the same two things decide your results.

Your photos do most of the work. On casual apps they do nearly all of
it, and most men lose here before anything else happens — bad
lighting, group shots, a lead photo that needs explaining.

Your bio does the filtering. It doesn't need to be clever; it needs
to be clear about what you're after so the wrong matches self-select
out. A short, specific, honest line beats a joke you borrowed. If
yours is doing neither, [the bio
guide](/blog/best-tinder-bios-for-guys) is the fastest fix on this
page.

Then there's the opener, which is where most matches quietly expire.
Casual intent doesn't mean crude — it means not pretending you're
after something else. Say something specific to their profile and
read what comes back. [Openers that get
replies](/blog/tinder-openers) covers the mechanics.

One thing worth saying plainly: reading intent is the skill, not
overcoming it. If someone's signals are ambiguous or they go quiet,
that's an answer. The people who do well at this are the ones who
move on quickly, not the ones who push. The off-app version of all of
this is in [how to hookup with a
girl](/blog/how-to-hookup-with-a-girl).

## The short version

Tinder if you want volume and your photos hold up. Feeld if you're in
a city and want intent stated openly. Hinge if you write better than
you photograph and don't mind a slower queue. Grindr if it applies to
you. Skip anything marketed purely as a hookup app.

Then stop app-shopping. Fix the lead photo, write a bio that filters,
and give one app four weeks. That sequence beats any ranking,
including this one.
