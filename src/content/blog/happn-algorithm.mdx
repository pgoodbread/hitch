Happn sells a simple promise: the people in your feed are people you
actually walked past. That's a nice story, and it's roughly true. It's
also why the happn algorithm confuses people more than Tinder's does —
when your feed goes quiet, you can't tell whether your profile is the
problem or your commute is.

Both are possible. Here's what the system is actually doing with your
location and your profile, how much of it is confirmed versus guessed
at, and which of it you can change.

## What the happn algorithm actually does

Happn's core mechanic is proximity over time. When your location is on,
the app records the points where you came near another member, and
those people turn up in your timeline. Happn's own support pages
describe this as the concept of the app, and third-party teardowns put
the crossing threshold at roughly 250 metres.

That changes what your feed is. On Tinder you're being ranked inside a
deck of everyone nearby. On Happn you're being ranked inside a much
smaller pool — the people whose physical path overlapped yours. The
pool is pre-filtered by your life before any software opinion about
your photos gets applied.

One detail matters here, because it breaks the clean version of the
story: Happn's help centre says it also shows you people you haven't
crossed paths with but might. So the feed isn't purely a log of real
encounters. It's a mix of actual crossings and predicted ones, and
Happn doesn't publish the ratio.

A like that gets liked back is a Crush, and a Crush is what opens the
chat. Happn's app-store copy states the principle plainly — you won't
get a message from someone you didn't show interest in. Free accounts
can send likes, chat with Crushes, and play CrushTime, the grid game
that asks you to guess which of four profiles already liked you.

## What counts as crossing paths

Three things decide how many people land in your timeline, and only one
of them is about you being attractive.

- **How much you move.** Someone commuting through a city centre
  crosses thousands of people a week. Someone working from home in a
  quiet suburb crosses dozens. Same profile, wildly different feed.
- **Where you move.** Density does the heavy lifting. Rush-hour
  stations, universities, gyms and nightlife areas put you in range of
  far more members than a retail park.
- **Whether location is actually on.** Background location permission
  is the whole engine. Restrict it and the app stops recording
  crossings, which looks identical to nobody being interested in you.

If your timeline is near-empty, check that third point before you start
rewriting your bio. On Happn, a permissions setting can be the entire
explanation.

The privacy side is better than the premise sounds, for what it's
worth. Happn doesn't hand anyone your live position — a crossing shows
the other person roughly where the overlap happened, not where you are
now or where you live. That's a deliberate design choice, and it's also
why the app can't tell you anything more precise than "somewhere around
here, at some point."

## The signals that seem to matter

Nobody outside the company has the ranking code, and any article giving
you an exact formula is making it up. What's reasonable to infer from
Happn's own descriptions and from how the app behaves:

- **Your crossing points.** The base layer. No overlap, no appearance.
- **Your stated preferences.** Age range, distance, who you're looking
  for — these cut the pool before anything else runs.
- **Your profile completeness.** More photos and a filled-out profile
  give viewers fewer reasons to swipe past, which is the number any
  ranking system ultimately reads.
- **Your activity.** This one is genuinely contested. Some of Happn's
  own material says matches are based on the other person's location
  rather than their activity; other descriptions say app activity feeds
  into what you're shown. Treat it as unsettled.

That last bullet is worth sitting with, because it's the honest state
of the evidence. Mozilla's review of the app noted it couldn't confirm
Happn's claims about AI-driven matching either. A lot of what circulates
as fact about the happn algorithm is marketing copy that got repeated
until it sounded like documentation.

## What Happn has never confirmed

Useful to know what isn't established, so you stop optimising for
ghosts:

- There's no published desirability score, no tier system, and no
  public evidence of one.
- There's no confirmed penalty for liking too many people, and no
  confirmed reward for being selective. People assume Happn works like
  [Tinder's ranking](/blog/tinder-algorithm) here. It might. Nobody
  outside the company knows.
- Deleting and reinstalling isn't a documented reset. It mostly costs
  you your Crushes and your chat history.

Paid tiers exist and they buy you reach and extra likes rather than a
different algorithm. Same logic as [Tinder Boost](/blog/tinder-boost):
you're multiplying however well your profile already converts, so it's
money well spent only after the profile works.

## Why you have no Crushes

Work through it in order, because the order saves you effort.

First, volume. If your timeline shows a handful of new people a day,
the problem is geography and permissions, not aesthetics. No photo
fixes a pool of twelve.

Second, conversion. If plenty of people are crossing your path and
nothing comes back, your profile is the bottleneck — and this is the
same failure mode as [getting no matches on
Tinder](/blog/why-am-i-not-getting-matches-on-tinder). The fix
transfers across apps because the judgement being made is identical:
someone looks at your first photo for a second and decides.

Third, the app itself. Happn is much smaller than the big swipe apps in
most cities, and in a small town the honest answer is that there aren't
enough members for the mechanic to work. That's worth checking against
[what other dating apps are good at](/blog/popular-dating-apps) before
you spend another month on it.

## What actually moves your numbers

The controllable half is the profile, and it's the same short list
everywhere.

Your first photo does most of the work.
[A clear, well-lit shot of your face](/blog/profile-pictures), no
sunglasses, no group, no cap pulled low. Every app shows that image
first and most people decide on it alone. If you change one thing this
week, change that.

After that, fill the profile out properly.
[A bio that gives someone something to react to](/blog/dating-profile-bio)
beats a list of adjectives, and an empty profile reads as a low-effort
account regardless of what the ranking does.

Then be consistent about where you are. Happn rewards being out in the
world in a way other apps don't — the same walk to work every day
builds a bigger crossing history than an app session on the sofa. It's
the one case where "just use the app more" is genuinely bad advice and
"leave the house" is the real lever.

## The short version

The happn algorithm filters by where you've physically been, then
shows you a mix of real and predicted crossings, then ranks what's left
by preferences and profile quality. Much of the detail is unpublished,
and people fill that gap with confident nonsense.

Which leaves two things that are definitely yours to control: how much
ground you cover with location switched on, and whether your first
photo and bio are good enough to convert the people who do see you.
Fix the second one and it pays off on every other app you use too.
