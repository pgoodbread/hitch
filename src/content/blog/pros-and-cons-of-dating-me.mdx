The pros and cons of dating me format spread because it looks easy.
Two short lists, one joke each, done. Then you open the box, type
"Pro: I'm loyal. Con: I'm always late," and realise you've written a
performance review instead of a bio.

The format works, but not for the reason most people think. It isn't a
joke delivery system. It's a self-awareness test, and the lists that
land pass it in about twenty words.

## What the pros and cons of dating me format is actually doing

Every other line in your bio is a claim about how great you are. This
one is the only place on a dating profile where you get to volunteer a
flaw, which makes it the only place she can calibrate everything else.

That's the mechanic. The "pros" aren't the point — she discounts them
automatically, the same way she discounts every other profile's. The
"cons" are the point. A real, small, specific con makes the pros
believable retroactively.

> ❌ Pro: loyal, funny, 6'2". Con: too honest.
>
> ✅ Pro: I will learn your coffee order by date two. Con: I will then
> tell people it's "our place."

The first one is a job application with a fake weakness attached. The
second one admits something slightly annoying and real, which is why
the pro above it reads as true.

## The formula

Three rules cover almost every good version of this.

- **One pair, not five.** Two pros and two cons is already a wall of
  text in a bio box. A single strong pair beats a list every time, and
  it leaves room for the rest of your profile.
- **The con has to actually cost you something.** "Con: I care too
  much" is a humblebrag in costume, and everyone reads it as one.
  "Con: I narrate every movie we watch" is a con. The test: would a
  friend who likes you name this if asked what you're like to be
  around?
- **Both halves need a picture.** Concrete nouns and specific
  behaviours, not adjectives. "Spontaneous" has no picture.
  "Suggesting a 9pm road trip on a Tuesday" does.

One more thing that separates the good ones: the pro and the con
should be the _same trait_ seen from two sides. That's what makes it
feel observed rather than assembled.

> ✅ Pro: I plan everything. Con: I plan everything.

Four words long, says more than a paragraph. Same trick, longer
version:

> ✅ Pro: I'll cook for you most nights. Con: you will hear the full
> story of where the recipe came from.

## Examples by type

Steal these, or use them as scaffolding. Each one names the kind of
person it fits, because a line that works on someone else's profile
can read as a lie on yours.

### The high-energy type

> Pro: I'll have us out the door before you've picked a jacket. Con: I
> genuinely thought 7am was a reasonable suggestion.

Fits anyone whose photos are already active. The con admits the thing
your gallery is implying anyway, which is what makes it funny instead
of defensive.

### The homebody

> Pro: I make a very good Sunday. Con: I will try to make Friday,
> Saturday and Thursday into Sundays as well.

Good if your photos are warm rather than adventurous. Most profiles
oversell how much they go out — being straight about it stands out and
filters correctly.

### The intense one

> Pro: I'm the person who remembers what you said three weeks ago. Con:
> I'm the person who remembers what you said three weeks ago.

The repeated-line version works best with traits that genuinely cut
both ways. It also reads fast, which matters in a bio.

### The funny one

> Pro: I'm told I'm very funny. Con: by me, mostly, and often.

Self-aware about the claim rather than making it. If humour is your
angle, see the [funny Tinder bios guide](/blog/funny-tinder-bios) for
the mechanics behind why the self-targeted version outperforms the
rest.

### The straight answer

> Pro: I text back. Con: usually three times in a row.

Not every bio needs a punchline. On an app full of ghosting, "I text
back" is a real pitch, and the con stops it from sounding smug.

### The niche one

> Pro: you'll never pick a bad restaurant again. Con: you'll hear why
> the bad ones are bad, in detail, outside them.

If you have a genuine obsession — food, climbing, records, a specific
game — run the formula through it. A vivid niche filters out people
who were never matching anyway.

## The versions that get you skipped

The failure modes are more predictable than the wins.

**The fake con.** "Con: I'm too generous." "Con: I work too hard."
"Con: I'm a perfectionist." These are the dating-app equivalent of
answering "what's your biggest weakness" in an interview. She's read
two hundred of them. A fake con doesn't just waste the line — it makes
the pros next to it read as equally manufactured.

**The real con.** There is a line, and the other side of it is worse.
"Con: I'm emotionally unavailable," "Con: I'm still kind of hung up on
my ex," "Con: I'm a lot to handle" — these are honest, and honesty
isn't the problem. The problem is that a bio has no room to give
context, so a serious flaw lands as a warning rather than a
confession. Keep the con charming and low-stakes. Save the real ones
for when there's a conversation to put them in.

**The résumé.** Five pros, two cons, bullet points, no jokes. It reads
as a listing. The format borrows its shape from a spec sheet, which is
exactly why it needs a human voice to work against — strip the voice
out and you've written an advert for yourself.

**The one that's about her.** "Con: your friends will be jealous."
"Pro: you'll finally have a guy who isn't a 5." Turning the con into a
compliment to yourself at someone's expense doesn't read as confident.
It's [negging](/blog/negging) with extra steps, and it has the same
hit rate.

The pattern underneath all four: the bad versions are written to be
admired. The good ones are written to be replied to.

## Making it fit a Tinder bio

The format was born on the prompt-based apps, where you get a labelled
box and a few lines of room. Tinder gives you one empty field and a
scroll that most people never reach, so it needs trimming.

**Cut to one pair.** On Hinge you can afford two pros and two cons. On
Tinder that's your entire bio spent on one joke.

**Lose the labels if they're eating space.** "Pro:" and "Con:" cost
eight characters and some of the rhythm. "I'll learn your coffee order
by date two, then tell people it's our place" does the same work in
one line.

**Don't let it repeat your photos.** If your gallery is four gym
mirrors, a con about being at the gym too much spends your bio
restating what she already saw. The bio's job is to show how you
_talk_ — the one thing photos can't do. That principle runs through
the whole [Tinder profile template](/blog/tinder-profile-template).

**Pick one hook and stop.** A pros-and-cons pair plus a "dating me is
like" line plus two more jokes is four punchlines fighting each other.
If you like both formats, the [dating me is like
guide](/blog/dating-me-is-like) covers the sibling version — use
whichever is stronger for you, not both.

## Write your own in five minutes

Borrowed lines are fine. Yours will be better, and it takes about as
long as scrolling for one.

1. Name one true, slightly inconvenient thing about being around you.
   Not a flaw — a texture. You over-order. You're always early. You
   have opinions about airports.
2. Find the upside of that same trait. There usually is one, and the
   pairing is what makes the line feel observed.
3. Write both halves as behaviours, not adjectives.
4. Cut it to under twenty-five words total.
5. Read it out loud. If it sounds like a caption, rewrite it until it
   sounds like a text.

Then leave it up for a week before judging it. One quiet evening is
not data, and swapping your bio every two days just means you never
learn which version worked. The broader structure this slots into is
covered in the [dating profile bio guide](/blog/dating-profile-bio),
and there are Tinder-specific examples in the [Tinder bios
guide](/blog/tinder-bios).

## The honest caveat

A great bio line is the second-most important thing in your profile,
and it isn't close. If your first photo doesn't earn the tap, [nobody
reads the bio at
all](/blog/why-your-first-tinder-photo-decides-everything). Fix the
lineup first, then let the line close.

If you're not sure which part is actually costing you matches — most
guys guess wrong — an [AI profile review](/) reads the photos, the
order and the bio together and tells you what to fix first.

## The takeaway

A good pros and cons of dating me answer is one pair, built from a
single trait seen two ways, written as behaviour instead of adjectives.
Skip the fake con, skip the serious one, skip the bullet-point résumé.

Get it right and it does what a bio is supposed to do: hand her
something easy to reply to. What you send after that is the [next
problem](/blog/dating-questions-to-ask).
