Every dry week on Tinder ends with the same theory: the algorithm is
punishing you. Somebody on Reddit has a fix, somebody on TikTok says
delete and reinstall, and the whole thing starts to feel like weather
you can't control.

The Tinder algorithm is less mysterious than that, and less personal.
It's a ranking system with one job — show each person the profiles
they're most likely to swipe right on, so they keep opening the app.
Everything it does to you follows from that. Once you see what it's
actually measuring, most of the folklore falls apart and a short list
of things worth changing is left standing.

## What the Tinder algorithm actually does

Tinder doesn't show you a random queue. It builds an ordered deck for
every session, and it does the same for everyone who could see you. The
question the system is answering is never "who deserves attention" —
it's "which card is this specific person most likely to like, right
now."

Tinder has said publicly that it stopped using the old internal
desirability score it ran for years. What replaced it leans on recent
activity: who you've liked lately, who's liked you, and how people
behave when your profile comes up. The system is looking for patterns
in swipes, not judging your face against a standard.

Two consequences matter. Your position isn't fixed — it moves with your
recent behaviour and recent results. And the ranking is relative to a
particular viewer, so there's no single number you can raise. You're
ranked differently in different decks.

## The signals it reads

Nobody outside the company has the source code, and any article claiming
an exact formula is guessing. But the inputs are visible enough in how
the app behaves.

- **Your swipe-right rate on you.** The single biggest one. Of the
  people shown your profile, how many like it? This is conversion, and
  it's the number your photos and bio control.
- **How selective you are.** Liking everything makes your likes
  worthless as a signal, and the system leans on your likes to
  understand your taste. Right-swiping the whole deck tells it nothing.
- **Recent activity.** Profiles that open the app get shown. Go quiet
  for three weeks and you fall out of the fresh pool, because the
  system would rather not send people to a match that never replies.
- **Reply behaviour after the match.** Matches that go nowhere are a
  bad outcome for the app. Conversations that run are a good one.
- **Profile completeness.** Six photos, a written bio and filled
  prompts give the system more to work with and give viewers fewer
  reasons to pass.
- **Distance, age filters, and who's actually nearby.** Plain mechanics,
  but they set the size of the pool before anything else applies.

Notice what isn't on that list: any lever you can pull without changing
the profile itself.

## The new-account boost is real, and it ends

New profiles get shown more. Tinder wants to learn where you fit and it
needs swipes to do it, so a fresh account gets pushed into decks quickly
and often has a strong first day or two.

That's why the delete-and-reinstall trick appears to work. It isn't a
reset of some punishment — it's a fresh learning period. The catch is
that the boost ends, and it ends at whatever rate your profile has
earned. If the underlying swipe-right rate is low, you burn through your
best distribution showing the same photos to more people, land back
where you started, and lose your matches and chat history on the way.

Tinder has also cracked down on serial account deletion, and reinstalls
increasingly restore the old account rather than creating a new one.
Treat it as a bad trade.

## What this means for your profile

Everything above collapses into one sentence: the algorithm amplifies
your conversion rate, it doesn't create it.

If 100 people see you and two swipe right, more distribution gets you
more twos. Pay for reach with [Tinder Gold](/blog/is-tinder-gold-worth-it)
and you multiply the same rate. Reinstall for a fresh boost and you
multiply the same rate. The rate is the thing.

So the useful work is at the front of the funnel:

**The first photo does most of it.** It's the only thing a lot of people
ever see before deciding.
[That one decision carries the profile](/blog/why-your-first-tinder-photo-decides-everything)
more than the other five combined, and it's the cheapest thing on this
list to fix.

**Fill all six slots.** Each empty slot is a missing chance to convert
someone who's undecided, and a thin profile reads as a low-effort one.

**Write a bio that gives her something to reply to.** A bio's job isn't
to summarise you, it's to hand over a hook.
[A bio that does something](/blog/best-tinder-bios-for-guys) beats a job
title and a height.

**Use a structure instead of guessing.** A
[profile template](/blog/tinder-profile-template) — a fixed photo order
with a job for each slot — gets you a competent profile in an afternoon.

**Swipe like it costs you something.** Being selective makes your likes
informative and keeps your own deck sane. If the vocabulary is still
fuzzy, the
[left swipe is worth understanding first](/blog/swipe-left-meaning).

## Things that don't work

- **Deleting and reinstalling.** Covered above. You trade your history
  for a boost your profile can't cash.
- **Mass right-swiping.** It degrades the signal the system uses to
  place you and fills your matches with people you won't message.
- **Swiping at a "magic hour".** Activity peaks exist — Sunday evenings
  are busy — but timing moves your numbers at the margin. It won't
  rescue a profile that isn't converting.
- **Paid tiers as a fix.** Boost and Super Likes buy visibility. They're
  a multiplier, not a repair.
- **Shadowban theories.** Slow weeks are usually a smaller local pool, a
  stale profile, or a rate that was always low. Actual restrictions come
  from reports and policy violations, and they're rare.

## How long a change takes to show up

Changing your first photo on a Tuesday and checking your matches on
Wednesday morning tells you almost nothing. The system needs swipes
before it can tell whether the new version converts better, and on a
normal account that's a few days of being shown to people.

Give any profile change about a week, and judge it on match rate rather
than match count — how many matches per hundred swipes you get, not the
raw total. Raw totals move with how much you swiped, how busy the app
was, and how big your local pool is, which makes them useless for
comparing one week to the next.

Change one thing at a time, too. Swapping all six photos and rewriting
the bio at once leaves you with a different result and no idea which
part caused it. The first photo is the highest-leverage single test, so
start there.

## Getting matches isn't the finish line

The algorithm reads what happens after the match too, and this is the
part people skip. Matches that sit unanswered are a dead end for you and
a bad outcome for the app.

Send a first message that's easy to answer.
[Openers that get replies](/blog/tinder-openers) are specific to her
profile and ask for something small. Generic "hey" messages are how a
good match rate turns into an empty inbox.

## The short version

The Tinder algorithm ranks your profile by how people respond to it, for
each viewer, weighted toward recent activity. It rewards profiles that
convert and accounts that show up. It has no grudge against you.

Which makes the fix boring and reliable: raise the swipe-right rate
first, stay reasonably active, be selective, and reply to your matches.
Then, if volume is the bottleneck, paying for reach becomes a choice
worth making — because you'd be multiplying something that works.
