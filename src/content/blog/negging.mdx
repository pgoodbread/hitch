Negging is a backhanded compliment used as a flirting tactic. You say
something that sounds like praise but lands as a small insult — "you'd
be really pretty if you smiled more" — on the theory that knocking
someone's confidence down a peg makes them work for your approval.

That's the definition. The rest of this article is the part that
matters more: it doesn't work, it hasn't worked for twenty years, and
on a dating app it fails faster than almost anything else you can
type.

## Negging, in one line

A neg is a criticism disguised as a compliment, delivered to someone
you're attracted to, on purpose.

Three things have to be true for it to count:

- **It's aimed at someone you want.** Insulting a stranger is just
  rudeness. Negging is specifically pointed at the person you're
  trying to attract.
- **It's disguised.** The insult is wrapped in something that gives
  the speaker deniability — a compliment, a joke, a "no offence but."
- **It's strategic.** The point isn't to be honest. The point is to
  make the other person feel slightly unsteady, so they seek
  reassurance from you.

That third one is what separates negging from ordinary teasing. Teasing
between two people who like each other is mutual and warm. A neg is
one-directional and calculated.

## Where the term came from

Negging comes out of the pickup-artist community of the early 2000s.
It was popularised by Erik von Markovik — who performed under the name
Mystery — and reached a mainstream audience through Neil Strauss's
2005 book *The Game*, which documented that scene from the inside.

The reasoning went like this: attractive women get complimented
constantly, so compliments are worthless currency. Withhold approval,
add a small dig, and you distinguish yourself from every other guy
while triggering a need to win you over.

It's a tidy theory. It's also built on a premise that falls apart the
moment you say it out loud: that the women you're interested in are a
category rather than individual people, and that they'll respond to a
technique rather than to you.

## What negging actually sounds like

The examples are worth seeing, because a lot of people neg without
knowing there's a word for it.

- "You're pretty for someone with such a weird laugh."
- "I normally don't go for girls like you, but there's something
  about you."
- "That's a bold outfit choice. I respect it."
- "You're smarter than you look."
- "Your nails are nice — are they real?"
- "You're funny. Most girls your age aren't."

Every one of these has the same architecture: a compliment carrying a
passenger. The compliment is the part you can point to if challenged.
The passenger is the part that actually gets remembered.

On dating apps the format has mutated into openers that lead with a
critique of her profile. "Cute, but your third photo is doing you no
favours." "Bio's a bit generic, but you seem alright." Same move,
faster delivery, and now it's the very first thing she knows about
you.

## Why negging backfires

The tactic was dubious in a bar in 2005. On an app in 2026 it's close
to a guaranteed loss, for reasons that are structural rather than
moral.

**She has options and no obligation.** In a bar, walking away from a
conversation has a small social cost. On Tinder it costs one thumb
movement and she never sees you again. Any opener that makes the next
thirty seconds unpleasant gets [a left swipe](/blog/swipe-left-meaning)
or an unmatch, and there's no recovery step.

**The word is public knowledge now.** *The Game* was twenty years ago.
Negging has been explained in magazines, on TikTok, in group chats.
Using it on someone who recognises it doesn't read as confidence — it
reads as someone running a script they found online, which is exactly
the impression you were trying to avoid.

**It signals the wrong thing about you.** The intended message is "I'm
selective and hard to impress." The received message is "this person
is going to be work." Nobody has ever read a backhanded compliment and
thought: I would like more of this over dinner.

**Contempt kills attraction faster than boredom does.** A dull opener
gets ignored. A dig gets you actively filed under avoid. Being
forgettable is a much cheaper mistake than being memorable for this.

**It preselects for exactly the wrong outcome.** In the rare case
where a neg does land, what it landed on was insecurity. You've
optimised for someone who responds to having their confidence
undermined. That is not a promising start to anything.

## If someone is negging you

Plenty of people arrive at this word from the other direction — a
conversation left a bad taste, they went looking for a name for it,
and found this one.

A useful test: after the exchange, do you feel better or slightly
worse about yourself? Genuine teasing leaves both people up. Negging
leaves one person marginally down, reliably, and the pattern repeats.

You don't owe anyone a debate about it. On an app, unmatching is a
complete response. If you want to name it, naming it plainly — "was
that meant to be a compliment?" — tends to end the technique
immediately, because it only functions while it stays deniable.

Worth knowing: this isn't only a dating-app behaviour. The same move
shows up in relationships and friendships, and there it's a much
bigger deal than a bad opener.

## What to do instead

The instinct behind negging isn't stupid. It's a real observation
wearing a bad solution. Generic compliments *are* weak openers. "Hey
beautiful" gets nothing, and correctly so — it's unanswerable, it
could have been sent to anyone, and it demonstrates that you looked at
one photo.

But the fix for a generic compliment is a specific observation, not an
insult. Both stand out. Only one of them makes her want to reply.

What actually works is boring:

1. **React to one specific thing.** A detail from her bio, a prompt
   answer, something identifiable in a photo. Specificity proves you
   read the profile, which is the entire signal a compliment was
   failing to send.
2. **Give her something to answer.** An opinion she can disagree with,
   a claim she can correct, a question with a real answer. This is the
   job the neg was badly trying to do — create a reason to respond.
3. **Keep it short and sound like yourself.** One or two lines. If the
   opener is wittier than you are, message three is where it collapses.

That's the whole alternative, and it's what people mean by
[rizz](/blog/rizz) when they use the word seriously. If you want
worked examples grouped by what you're reacting to, the
[rizz lines article](/blog/best-rizz-lines) is a set of them. And when
a conversation is actually going somewhere,
[asking her out](/blog/ask-girl-out-tinder) is the step most people
stall on.

## The uncomfortable part

Negging is usually a symptom. Guys reach for tactics when the ordinary
approach isn't producing results, and the ordinary approach usually
isn't producing results for a reason that has nothing to do with what
they type.

If you're getting matches and conversations die, your messaging is the
problem, and the three rules above fix most of it. If you're barely
getting matches at all, no opener is reaching anyone — the problem is
upstream, in your photos and your
[bio](/blog/best-tinder-bios-for-guys). No line, clever or cruel,
compensates for a profile that doesn't earn the right swipe.

Most people guess wrong about which of the two they have. If you'd
rather not guess, an [AI profile review](/) grades your photos, their
order and your bio together and tells you which half is actually
costing you matches.

## The takeaway

Negging is a backhanded compliment used as a flirting tactic. It came
from the pickup-artist scene of the early 2000s, was popularised by
*The Game*, and was built on the idea that undermining someone's
confidence makes them chase you.

It doesn't work on dating apps. She has no reason to stay in an
unpleasant conversation, the tactic is widely recognised, and in the
rare case it lands, it landed on the wrong thing.

The real problem it was trying to solve — generic compliments are
weak — is genuine. The fix is being specific, not being mean.
