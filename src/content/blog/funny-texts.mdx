The problem with funny texts is that the ones people save and reuse are
the ones that stopped being funny about four thousand sends ago. She
has seen the pickup line about the lost phone number. She has seen the
one that opens with a fake customer service greeting. They were good
once, which is exactly why they are dead now.

What survives is smaller and much less impressive on paper. A text
lands as funny when it's short, when it's about something in front of
both of you, and when answering it is easy. Most of the material below
is built around those three things rather than around jokes, because
jokes are the part people get wrong.

## Why most funny texts die on arrival

Three reasons, and none of them are about the punchline.

The first is length. A joke that needs a setup paragraph isn't a text,
it's a bit, and a bit performed over SMS reads as effort. Effort is the
thing you're trying not to show. If the funny part arrives in the third
sentence, the first two already gave the game away.

The second is that it doesn't give her anything to do. "Haha that's so
true 😂" is a dead end — you made a joke, she acknowledged the joke,
now what. The funny texts that keep a thread alive have a handle on
them: an accusation she can deny, a wrong guess she can correct, a
fake-serious verdict she can argue with. She needs a move, not an
audience seat.

The third is timing, and it's the one that quietly kills the most
threads. A funny text into a warm conversation is continuation. The
same text sent at 11pm into a thread that stopped on Sunday is a
stranger doing stand-up. Same words, completely different read.

## Four situations, and what works in each

### The first message after a match

The bar here is lower than most guys think, because the competition is
"hey" and "how's your weekend going." You don't need to be hilarious.
You need to be the one message in her stack that was clearly written
after looking at her profile.

The format that works is a wrong guess, stated with confidence:

- "Okay the hiking photo is a trap. You've been up one mountain and
  you're going to make it a personality for the rest of the year."
- "Three dog pictures and no human ones. I'm choosing to believe the
  dog made this profile."
- "Your bio says you're competitive. About what. Be specific, I need to
  know what I'm agreeing to."

Each of these is one line, each is about her specifically, and each
hands her something to push back on. That's the whole design. If you
want the broader version of this — openers that aren't going for funny
at all — the [opener patterns](/blog/tinder-openers) cover the ground
that humour doesn't need to.

### Reviving a thread that died

The dead-thread text is the hardest one to get right, because anything
that acknowledges the silence makes the silence bigger. "Hey stranger"
and "did you fall off the earth" both point at the thing you want her
to not think about.

The move is to come back as if nothing happened, with something that
only makes sense because of the earlier conversation:

- "Update on the thing you said about airport sandwiches: you were
  right and I'm furious about it."
- "I just saw someone do the exact thing you complained about last week
  and I thought of you, which is probably not a compliment."

Two things are doing the work. You're referencing her, which proves you
remembered, and you're arriving with news rather than a request. No
question mark needed. If she's interested, the news is enough.

### Replying to a dry answer

She gave you four words and no question. Most guys respond by asking
something earnest, which is how threads turn into interviews.

Better: name the dryness, lightly, and move on in the same text.

- "That was the most efficient sentence anyone's sent me today. Anyway
  — "
- "I'm reading that as either very busy or very unimpressed and I'd
  rather not know which."

You're teasing the format, not her, and you're not asking her to
explain herself. Then you change the subject in the same breath so
there's somewhere to go. The one thing that doesn't work is doubling
the energy — a long enthusiastic text aimed at a short flat one widens
the gap, it doesn't close it.

### Recovering from a send that flopped

It happens. The joke didn't land, or it landed wrong, and now you're
looking at a reply that's noticeably cooler than the last one.

Self-deprecation is the only clean exit, and it has to be brief:

- "That was funnier in my head and I'd like it struck from the record."
- "Rough one. I'm going to pretend that was autocorrect."

Then stop. The recovery text works because it's one line and then
silence. Explaining the joke, or apologising for it twice, turns a bad
text into a bad conversation.

## The five ways guys kill it

**Joke stacking.** The first one didn't get a reply, so you send
another. Now there are two unanswered jokes sitting there and the third
one is going to be read with real hostility. One joke, then wait.

**Punching down.** Teasing her for something she chose is fine. Teasing
her for something she didn't — her job, her height, her hometown, her
photos of her own face — is not teasing, it's just an insult with a
smile on it. The line is whether she can plausibly hit back.

**Needing the laugh.** "😂😂" after your own joke, "no but seriously,"
"was that too much" — these are all the same text, and the text says
please confirm I'm funny. Confident indifference to whether it landed
is most of what makes it land.

**Copy-paste material.** Canned lines have a specific smell: too
polished for the thread they're in. The same thing is true one step
earlier, in your profile — a [funny bio](/blog/funny-tinder-bios) works
for exactly the same reason a funny text does, and fails for the same
reason when it's borrowed.

**Volume as a substitute.** If every text is a bid for a laugh, none of
them are. Humour works as contrast. Mix it with the register that
actually moves things forward — the
[flirty text](/blog/flirty-texts-for-her) does a different job, and a
thread needs both.

## Read the reply before you send the next one

The response to a funny text tells you how much room you have, and it's
usually legible.

She adds to the joke: you're in, keep the pace. She laughs and asks
something: also in, and she's taken over steering, which is better. She
laughs and stops: polite, not engaged — change register, ask about her.
A reaction emoji with no words: the thread is cooling, give it a day.

The ambiguous ones are where guys invent stories. If she trails off
with an ellipsis, that [means something specific](/blog/meaning-in-text-from-a-girl)
and it's rarely what you're hoping. Same with a reply that arrives
twelve hours later — it's information about her interest level, not a
puzzle about her schedule.

And if you're texting someone in the morning, the humour mostly
shouldn't be there at all; the
[good morning text](/blog/good-morning-text-for-him) works on warmth,
not on jokes.

## The short version

Funny texts work when they're one line, about her specifically, and
easy to answer. Make a wrong guess she can correct, tease the format
instead of the person, and send it into a thread that's already warm.

Then put the phone down. The restraint after the joke is doing as much
work as the joke, and it's the part almost nobody gets right.
