Tinder hookups are the thing the app is best known for and the thing
it explains worst. Nobody tells you whether casual is still on the
menu, where you are allowed to say so, or why the guy with worse
photos than you is apparently doing fine.

The honest version: it still works, and almost none of the work
happens in the chat. By the time you are typing, the outcome is
mostly decided. Your profile picked who you matched with, and who you
matched with decides what is possible.

This is the Tinder-specific version. The broader, off-app view of the
same goal is in [how to hookup with a girl](/blog/how-to-hookup-with-a-girl).

## Do Tinder hookups still happen?

Yes. What changed is that the app stopped being one undifferentiated
pool of people.

Tinder now asks users what they are looking for, and a lot of them
answer honestly — relationship, something casual, still figuring it
out. That field is the single most useful thing on the app and most
guys ignore it completely. It means you are no longer guessing. A
meaningful share of the people you see have already told you whether
casual is on the table.

This reframes the whole problem. You are not trying to talk someone
into something. You are trying to be visible and appealing to the
people whose stated intent already matches yours. Those are very
different jobs, and only one of them has a decent success rate.

The guys who think Tinder is dead for this are usually doing the
first job — swiping on everyone, then trying to convert whoever
replies. That fails constantly, and it fails in a way that feels like
rejection when it is really just a mismatch you could have read in
advance.

## Your profile picks your matches

Here is the part that stings: if your matches never go anywhere, the
problem is upstream of your messages.

Think about what a right swipe actually means on the other side. She
saw your first photo, maybe three more, maybe two lines of bio, and
decided in under two seconds. Everything she believes about you at
message one came from that. If the profile says nothing in
particular, you get matches from people who were swiping on
autopilot, and those convert at roughly zero regardless of what you
send.

The fix is boring and it is the whole game:

- **First photo does the deciding.** Clear face, good light, no
  sunglasses, no group shot. If someone has to work out which one is
  you, you have already lost.
- **Give four to six, not nine.** Each weak photo drags the average
  down. People judge the set, not the best one in it.
- **One full-body shot.** Leaving it out reads as hiding something,
  and for casual specifically, people want to know what they are
  agreeing to meet.
- **Show a life, not a highlight reel.** Something you actually do
  beats a rented supercar every time.

None of that is about looking like a model. It is about looking like
a specific person who put in ten minutes of effort, because the
baseline you are competing against is men who put in none.

## Saying what you want without being crude

This is where most profiles blow it in one direction or the other.

One group says nothing, hopes it comes up, and ends up in week-long
chats with people who wanted something else entirely. The other group
writes the bio equivalent of a billboard, which gets them left-swiped
by nearly everyone including the people who would otherwise have been
interested.

The middle is simple. Set the intent field honestly, then let your
bio be about you rather than about your availability. Stated intent
belongs in the structured field, where it is read as information.
Stated intent in the bio text reads as a pitch, and pitches are
uncomfortable.

What the bio is for is giving someone a reason to reply and a sense
of whether you would be fun for an evening. Humour does that better
than anything else, partly because it signals you are relaxed about
the whole thing — which is itself the attractive quality here.
[Funny Tinder bios](/blog/funny-tinder-bios) are not a gimmick for
this reason, and if you want the structural version,
[the bios that work for guys](/blog/best-tinder-bios-for-guys) covers
what to put where.

Two things to avoid specifically:

- **Negativity.** "No drama", "swipe left if", any list of what you
  do not want. It reads as a man with a grievance.
- **Pretending to want a relationship.** It buys you matches that
  waste everyone's time, and it is a genuinely unpleasant thing to do
  to someone. Mismatched intent discovered on date two is worse for
  both of you than a smaller match count ever was.

Honest and light outperforms both of them, every time.

## The chat has a short runway

Tinder conversations have a shelf life of roughly two to four days of
active messaging. After that her matches stack up, the novelty wears
off, and the chat that was fun on Tuesday is admin by Sunday.

For casual specifically the runway is shorter, not longer. A long
text relationship actively works against it — the more the whole
thing lives in the chat, the more meeting feels like a big escalation
rather than the obvious next step. Momentum is the thing you are
protecting.

Which means the common anxieties here mostly do not matter. Whether
she has seen the message, how long to wait before replying, whether
double texting is fatal. If you find yourself staring at the thread
doing forensics, [read receipts on Tinder](/blog/does-tinder-have-read-receipts)
explains what the app does and does not tell you, and the short
answer is that none of it is worth your attention.

What is worth your attention is whether she is investing. Longer
replies than yours, questions coming back, logistics volunteered
without prompting. Two of those and you have something real. None of
them and you have a polite conversation, whatever the message count
says.

## Move to meeting early and keep it small

The mistake is almost never moving too fast. It is waiting for a
certainty that never shows up.

If the investment signals are there on day two, that is your moment.
Ask for one concrete, small, public thing — a drink on a named
evening, not "we should do something sometime". A sentiment is not a
question, and leaving her to design the whole plan is how good chats
quietly die. The full version of the ask is in
[how to ask a girl out on Tinder](/blog/ask-girl-out-tinder).

A hookup is not the plan for the first meeting. It is a possible
outcome of one, and treating it as the plan is exactly what makes men
weird and women cautious. Short and public also happens to be in your
interest: forty minutes settles a question that four more days of
texting will not, and it gives both of you a clean exit if there is
nothing there.

If it is going well she will usually make that obvious. Suggesting
the next thing is fine. Assuming it is not. And a soft no — "maybe!",
a topic change, a reply that never comes — is an answer. Take it the
first time. Re-asking converts almost nobody and costs you the
goodwill you built.

## Does paying for it help?

Only if match volume is genuinely your bottleneck, and for most guys
it is not.

Boosts and subscriptions buy you more eyes on the same profile. If
that profile is converting badly, you are paying to show a weak
profile to more people, which gets you more of the same result at a
monthly cost. Fix the photos first and see what your actual match
rate is. If you are then getting decent matches and simply want more
of them, [Tinder Gold](/blog/is-tinder-gold-worth-it) is a reasonable
thing to consider on those terms.

That order matters. Profile, then volume. Never the reverse.

## The takeaway

Tinder hookups come down to four things, and only one of them is
about messaging:

1. **Match with people whose intent already matches yours.** The app
   tells you. Read it.
2. **Make the profile do the selling.** Four to six strong photos, a
   bio with a personality in it.
3. **Be honest and light about what you want.** Structured field, not
   the bio text.
4. **Move while it is warm, ask small, take a no cleanly.**

The uncomfortable part is that most of the leverage sits before
anyone types anything. If you would rather not guess which end of
yours is broken, an [AI profile review](/) looks at the photos, the
order and the bio together and tells you what to fix first.

Everything after that is mostly not being weird about it.
