Every app gives you a box and almost no instruction. Some call it a
bio, some call it an About Me, some break it into prompts — but the
job is the same everywhere, and most people write a dating profile bio
that would work equally well on anyone else's profile. That's the
whole problem in one sentence.

This is the version that isn't about one app. What the box is actually
doing, the structure that transfers everywhere, how length changes
results, and the handful of lines that sink an otherwise fine profile.

## What a dating profile bio is actually for

It isn't to attract anyone. The photos do that, and they do it before
the text gets a single second of attention. By the time anyone reads
your bio they've already decided you're worth a closer look.

Which means the bio has exactly one job: convert interest that already
exists into a swipe, and ideally into something to say.

That reframe matters because it lowers the bar in a useful way. Your
bio doesn't need to be funny. It doesn't need to be impressive. It
needs to answer two questions quickly — what kind of person is this,
and is there anything here I could reply to?

Miss the first and you read as generic. Miss the second and you get
matches that never turn into conversations, which feels like progress
and isn't.

Worth saying plainly before anything else: if your photos aren't
earning the tap, no bio will rescue the profile, because it never gets
read. [Profile pictures that get matches](/blog/profile-pictures)
covers that side, and it's the one to fix first.

## The structure that works on any app

Strip away the app-specific formatting and working bios tend to have
three parts, in this order.

**One line that says who you are.** Not your job title. Something with
a texture to it — how you spend a Saturday, the thing you're
unreasonably committed to, where you actually live versus where you
say you live.

**One line that's specific enough to be yours alone.** This is where
most profiles collapse. "I love travel, food and good music" is true
of nearly everyone holding a phone. "I will drive two hours for decent
pho" is true of you. Specificity isn't decoration — it's the only
thing making you distinguishable from the forty profiles after yours.

**One line that leaves an opening.** A mild opinion, an invitation, a
question worth arguing with. A bio that explains everything is a closed
door. One slightly odd detail gives someone a reply that writes itself.

Three lines. That's a complete bio. Anything else you add is competing
with those three for attention, and usually losing.

## How long should a dating profile bio be?

Shorter than you think, and the reason isn't attention spans.

Two to four lines is the working range. People read profiles mid-scroll
with a thumb already moving, so the fourth line is roughly where
reading stops regardless of what's there. Writing six good lines means
two of them are decoration and the best one might be buried in the
part nobody reached.

The common failure in the other direction is a one-word bio, or an
empty box, or a single emoji. That reads as low effort even when it's
meant to read as confident, and low effort is the one signal that
costs you matches with people who were otherwise interested.

If you genuinely can't fill three lines, one specific true line beats
three vague ones. Just don't leave it blank.

## Free-text boxes versus prompt-based profiles

Apps split into two shapes, and the same bio doesn't fit both.

**Free-text** is a blank box and total freedom, which is harder, not
easier. Nothing stops you from writing a paragraph about yourself in
the third person. The three-part structure above is for this case.
Tinder is the one most people mean, and
[Tinder bios that actually get matches](/blog/tinder-bios) goes
deeper on that specific version.

**Prompt-based** apps hand you a question and a small box. The trap
here is answering the question literally and completely. "Two truths
and a lie" answered carefully is a quiz nobody asked to take. The
prompt is a frame for showing character, not a form to fill in, and
the best answers are short, concrete, and slightly unexpected.

The format cuts across both, too — a sentence stem you complete, like
[dating me is like](/blog/dating-me-is-like), works in a free-text
box just as well as it does as a prompt answer.

One rule covers both shapes: every line should be something only you
could have written. If a stranger could copy it onto their profile
unchanged, it isn't doing any work on yours.

## The lines that kill a profile

A few patterns reliably cost matches, and they show up constantly.

- **Lists of adjectives.** Ambitious, funny, laid back, loyal. Everyone
  claims these and nobody can verify them. Show one, claim none.
- **Rules and filters.** "No hookups," "swipe left if…," "don't bother
  if you can't hold a conversation." Even when fair, these read as
  tired before the first message, and they're aimed at people who
  already swiped past you.
- **Shots at the app or at other people on it.** Complaining about
  dating apps on a dating app, or about an entire gender, says more
  about you than about them. Self-aware lands. Aggrieved doesn't.
- **Height, salary, and what you drive**, stated flatly. If it matters
  to someone they'll find it out. Leading with it reads as a negotiating
  position.
- **Borrowed lines.** The copied joke from a listicle has been seen
  forty times this week, and it signals someone who outsourced the
  one part of the profile that was supposed to be theirs.

Humour is the exception people get wrong in both directions. A funny
bio works, but only when the joke is yours and the profile can survive
the joke falling flat —

longer version of that argument. If you want worked examples to react
to rather than rules,

the concrete ones.

## Write yours in ten minutes

1. **List five true, specific things about yourself.** Not impressive.
   True and oddly specific. The opinion you can't let go of. The hobby
   you're genuinely mediocre at.
2. **Delete the three that sound like anyone else.** Travel, food,
   music, dogs. All real, all useless as signal.
3. **Keep two and write them as plain sentences.** No jokes yet.
4. **Make the last line answerable.** An opinion, a small provocation,
   an invitation. Something a reply can attach to.
5. **Read it as a stranger.** If the reaction is "sure, fine," it isn't
   finished. If it's "wait, what?", it is.

Then leave it alone for two weeks before you touch it again. Rewriting
every few days means you never learn which version actually worked.

## The takeaway

A dating profile bio isn't a personality test or a joke you borrow.
It's three lines doing one job: proving you're a specific person and
giving someone an easy reason to say something.

Write who you are, write one detail only you could claim, leave one
opening, and stop. That beats every copied line scrolled past this
morning.

And keep the bio's win in proportion — it buys a match, not a date.
