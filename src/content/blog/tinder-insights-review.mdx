You've had a slow month, you've started wondering whether the app is
hiding you, and somewhere in that spiral you found a site offering to
turn your Tinder data into charts. This Tinder Insights review is for
that moment — the one where numbers feel like they'd finally explain
what's going wrong.

The short answer: the stats are real, they're mildly interesting, and
they will not tell you the thing you actually want to know. They
measure the symptom. They can't name the cause.

## What people mean by Tinder Insights

This is the first thing worth clearing up, because the name points at
two different things and most of the confusion starts here.

**Third-party stats tools.** This is what the search is usually
about. Tinder lets you request a copy of your own data — swipes,
matches, messages, app activity, going back as far as your account
does. A handful of outside sites take that file and render it as
charts: swipes sent, match rate, reply rate, how your activity moves
across the week, sometimes a comparison against other people who
uploaded theirs. Tinder supplies the file. Somebody else builds the
dashboard.

**Tinder's own photo suggestions.** Separately, Tinder has shipped
features that nudge you about your photos — picking out images from
your camera roll it thinks would do well. That's a Tinder product, it
lives in the app, and it has nothing to do with uploading a data
export anywhere. Availability moves around by account and region, so
if you don't see it, that's normal rather than a sign something's
broken.

The rest of this is about the first kind, because that's the one
people are deciding whether to use.

## What the numbers actually show you

The headline stat is your match rate: right swipes sent versus matches
back. Most tools put that front and centre, usually next to a
benchmark drawn from everyone else who uploaded.

Below that you get the activity picture. How many swipes you've sent
in total, how that's trended over months, what share of matches turned
into a conversation, what share of conversations made it past the
second message. Some tools break it down by the days and hours you're
most active.

Two of those are genuinely useful, and they're not the one you came
for.

**Where conversations die is a real finding.** If you're matching fine
and almost nothing survives past message two, that's a specific
problem with a specific fix, and it isn't your photos. Knowing the
drop-off is at the opener rather than at the match is worth the
upload on its own.

**Your own volume is usually a surprise.** People badly misjudge how
much they swipe. Guys convinced the app has throttled them often find
they sent a few hundred right swipes in a month, which is a thin
sample to draw any conclusion from. That reframe is useful even when
nothing else is.

The match rate itself, though, is the stat everyone fixates on and the
one that travels worst.

## What a Tinder Insights review can't tell you

Your match rate is one number summarising your city, your age bracket,
your photos, your bio, how liberally you swipe, and how active the
local pool is — all mashed together. It moves when any of those move,
which means it can't isolate any of them.

That cuts the legs off the comparison feature. A benchmark built from
people who cared enough about their dating stats to go find a website
and upload a data file is not a sample of Tinder. It skews toward the
invested and the frustrated. Being above or below that line tells you
roughly nothing about where you sit among people actually swiping near
you.

And the question you want answered — *which photo is costing me* — is
exactly the one the export can't reach. Tinder doesn't record who saw
your profile, which photo they stopped on, or where they swiped away.
None of that is in the file, so none of it can be in the chart. A
dashboard built on your data can show you the score. It has no access
to the game.

That's the whole limitation, and it's structural, not a gap some
better tool will close later. If you want to know why the right
swipes aren't landing, you need to look at
[the profile itself](/blog/why-am-i-not-getting-matches-on-tinder),
not at a tally of what it earned.

## The privacy trade

Worth being clear-eyed about, because it's the real cost here rather
than the money.

The export you'd be uploading is not a tidy summary. It's your
message history, your match list, your location and usage patterns,
and a long record of who you swiped on and when. You're handing all of
it to a site you found through a search result, on the strength of a
privacy policy you almost certainly didn't read.

Most of these tools are run by small operations, and plenty of them
sell a paid product on the other side of the free chart. That isn't
automatically sinister — it's just the business model, and it's worth
knowing it's the business model before you decide the chart is free.

If you do it: open the file first and look at what's in it. Prefer a
tool that processes in your browser rather than uploading to a server,
if you can tell the difference. And delete the account afterwards. If
none of that appeals, you're giving up a couple of numbers you could
mostly estimate by hand.

## What to do instead

The useful version of this exercise takes an afternoon and doesn't
involve giving anyone your messages.

Count by hand for two weeks. Right swipes sent, matches back,
conversations that got past the opener. Three numbers, a note on your
phone. That's the same diagnosis the dashboard offers, at the only
resolution that matters: is the leak at the match, or after it?

If it's after the match, the fix is in your messaging, and it's
usually the first line.

If it's at the match — and for most guys it is — the leak is the
profile, and the photos carry almost all of it. The first image is the
only one a lot of people ever see, so it decides the swipe on its own.
That's where the honest testing happens: change one photo, swipe a
comparable amount, see whether the rate moves.
[Getting the photos right](/blog/profile-pictures) moves the number
further than any analysis of the number will. Then
[the bio](/blog/tinder-bios), which closes people who paused rather
than winning the ones who didn't.

It's also worth knowing that your rate isn't purely a verdict on you.
[How the algorithm distributes profiles](/blog/tinder-algorithm) means
a quiet stretch can be the pool thinning out, not your profile
degrading. One slow fortnight is noise.

## The short version

Tinder Insights tools do what they claim. They read your export and
draw honest charts of your own activity, and the conversation drop-off
number is a real diagnostic you won't get elsewhere. If you're
curious and relaxed about the privacy trade, it's a free afternoon.

But treat the match rate as a thermometer, not an x-ray. It tells you
something's off without telling you what, the benchmark it compares
you against is a self-selected crowd, and the one question you're
really asking — which photo is losing you matches — is not answerable
from a data export at all.

The numbers always point back at the profile. You can get there
directly and skip the upload, in the same way that
[paying Tinder for more reach](/blog/is-tinder-gold-worth-it) only
pays off once the profile is worth showing to more people. Fix the
photos, then the bio, then measure. In that order the counting is
worth doing, because by then there's something to count.
