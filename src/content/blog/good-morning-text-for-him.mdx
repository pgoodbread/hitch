A good morning text for him is not a greeting. It's the cheapest signal
of attention you can send: proof that he was the first thing you thought
about, delivered at the one hour of the day when nobody has anything
better to do than look at their phone.

That's also why the generic ones fail. "Good morning 😊" sent every day
at 8am stops being a signal after about a week — it becomes wallpaper,
something he swipes past on the way to his email. The texts that work
say something. They give him a reason to open the thread rather than
just notice it.

## What a good morning text for him actually does

Three things, and it only needs to do one of them well.

- **It reopens a conversation.** Most threads don't die from conflict.
  They die because both people got busy at the same time and nobody
  restarted. A morning text is the lowest-effort restart there is.
- **It puts you in his day early.** Not in a manipulative way — people
  genuinely think about whoever they spoke to first. Eight in the
  morning is uncontested territory.
- **It tells him where you stand.** Early on, most people are guessing
  about how interested the other person is. A morning text answers that
  question without you having to say anything out loud.

Notice what isn't on that list: being clever. A morning text is not the
place for your best material. It's the place for warmth plus one
specific detail.

## The four patterns that work

Almost every morning text worth sending is one of these four. Learn the
shape and you never need a list of a hundred again.

### The callback

You reference something from the last conversation. This is the
highest-hit-rate morning text there is, because it proves you were
still thinking about what he said after you put your phone down.

> Woke up still annoyed on your behalf about that meeting. Did he
> actually apologise or did he just move on like nothing happened?

> Morning. I looked up that band you mentioned and I owe you an
> apology, they're excellent.

> Still thinking about your take on pineapple pizza. Sleep did not
> change my position.

The structure is simple: name the thing, add your reaction, leave an
opening. He doesn't have to invent a topic — you handed him one he
already cares about.

### The plan-setter

You attach the text to something happening in his day. It reads as
interest in his actual life rather than a generic pulse check.

> Good morning — isn't today the presentation? You're going to be fine.
> Tell me how it goes.

> Morning. Big day or a "stare at spreadsheets until lunch" day?

> Hope the early start isn't as brutal as you made it sound. What time
> do you actually escape today?

The payoff comes later: you've built in a reason to text again this
evening, and it won't feel like you're checking up on him.

### The question with a real answer

A morning text with a question attached gets a reply roughly twice as
often, but only if the question is answerable before coffee. Keep it
light and keep it about him.

> Morning. Settle something for me — are you a snooze person or one of
> those people who gets up on the first alarm? I need to know what I'm
> dealing with.

> Good morning. What's the first thing you do when you wake up, and be
> honest, is it your phone?

> Morning — quick one. Coffee or nothing until 11?

Anything that needs thirty seconds of thought gets answered "later",
which usually means never. If you want the longer version of what
separates a question that lands from one that dies, we've written about
[questions to ask your crush](/blog/question-to-ask-your-crush)
elsewhere.

### The specific compliment

Compliments work in the morning because they arrive without context and
without an ask attached. The rule is the same one that governs all
flirting: compliment something he chose, not something he was born with.

> Morning. Random, but you're genuinely funny and I don't say that
> about many people.

> Good morning. Still impressed you drove two hours for your sister's
> thing. That says a lot about you.

> Morning — you were right about the film and I hate it.

"You're so handsome" sent at 7am is fine, but it's forgettable.
"You're the only person who's made me laugh this week" isn't. If you
want to calibrate how playful to go before you know him well, the
examples in our [rizz lines guide](/blog/best-rizz-lines) are written
the other way round, but the tone rules carry over exactly.

## Timing beats content

A good text at the wrong hour underperforms a mediocre one at the right
hour.

Send between 7 and 9am on a workday. He's awake, commuting or eating
breakfast, and he has his phone in his hand with nothing to do. Before
6am reads as "I didn't sleep". After 10am it isn't a morning text
anymore, it's just a text.

Weekends shift two hours later. A 7am Saturday text lands while he's
asleep, gets read at eleven in a pile of notifications, and loses
everything that made it feel personal.

And don't send one every single day. Two or three mornings a week keeps
it meaningful. Daily turns it into a routine, and routines stop being
noticed — which is the exact opposite of what you were going for.

## The three ways it goes wrong

- **The paragraph.** Four sentences about how you slept, what you're
  wearing and what you dreamt about is a lot to process at 7am. He'll
  save it for later and reply to something easier first. Two lines,
  maximum.
- **The daily "gm".** Same word, same time, every day. It costs you
  nothing to send and tells him nothing, so it earns nothing back.
- **Warmth that hasn't been earned yet.** "Good morning gorgeous, hope
  you slept well 🥰" after two days of talking is a mismatch, and
  mismatches make people pull back. Warmth should track the actual
  temperature of the thing.

There's a fourth, subtler one: the morning text that's actually a test.
If you're sending it to see whether he replies, he'll feel the weight
on it, and the thread gets heavier for both of you.

## How to read the reply

This is where most people get it wrong, so be specific about what each
reply actually means.

**A long or fast reply** means the door is open. Don't celebrate by
sending four more texts — answer the thing he asked, then let it
breathe until the evening. Momentum is something you spend, not
something you hoard.

**A short reply** usually means he's busy, not uninterested. "Morning,
crazy day, talk later" is a normal message from someone at work. The
correct response is nothing, until later. People who send a follow-up
into a short reply almost always make the thread worse.

**Silence until the afternoon** means very little on its own. Once is
noise. Three morning texts in a row that get a shrug is a pattern, and
patterns are worth believing — at that point you stop sending them and
see whether he starts.

If you're tempted to draw conclusions from whether he's seen it, know
that the read-receipt signal is much weaker than people assume. We went
through what it does and doesn't tell you in
[does Tinder have read receipts](/blog/does-tinder-have-read-receipts).

## When the morning text is doing too much work

A morning text is maintenance. It keeps a warm thing warm. It cannot
start something that isn't there, and it cannot rescue a conversation
that's been one-sided for a fortnight.

If it's been going well for a couple of weeks and you're still texting,
the morning text has done its job and the next move is a plan. Attaching
it to the text you're already sending is the easiest version:

> Morning. Also, that place you mentioned on Thursday — are we going or
> are we just going to keep talking about it?

The timing logic there is the same on both sides of the app. Our guide
to [asking someone out on Tinder](/blog/ask-girl-out-tinder) is written
from the other direction, but the part that matters — ask while the
conversation is still good, not after it's cooled — applies to you
exactly as written.

## The takeaway

A good morning text for him needs one specific thing in it: a callback,
a detail from his day, an easy question, or a compliment about
something he chose. Send it between 7 and 9am, keep it to two lines,
and skip a few days so it keeps meaning something.

Then read the reply honestly. Long means keep going, short means wait,
and a pattern of nothing means the thread was never really there.
